<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:6.0pt;text-align:justify'><b><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>I.  Luke 22:35-38</span></b></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>In Luke’s gospel Jesus gave
his disciples one final piece of advice before they left the upper room for
Gethsemane: </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify;text-indent:.5in'><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt;line-height:115%'>35</span></sup></b><i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>“When I sent you out with no money
bag, knapsack, or shoes, you didn’t lack anything, did you? “No.” </span></i><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt;line-height:115%'>36</span></sup></b><i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>“But now, if you have a money bag,
take it—and a knapsack too. If you don’t have a sword, sell your coat and buy
one. </span></i><b><sup><span style='font-size:14.0pt;line-height:115%'>37</span></sup></b><i><u><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>I</span></u></i><i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'> have to fulfill what this scripture
says about me: <b>‘They treated me like a criminal’</b></span></i><b><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'> </span></b><b><span
style='font-size:9.0pt;line-height:115%'>[Is 53:12]</span></b><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>.<b><i> </i></b><i>What refers to me
will happen.” </i></span><b><sup><span style='font-size:14.0pt;line-height:
115%'>38</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>“Lord,
here are two swords.” “That’s enough.”</span></i><span style='font-size:12.0pt;
line-height:115%'>        </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>In the future, extending
the kingdom of God will be different from what these men had experienced in the
Mission of the Twelve (Luke <b>9</b>:1-3 = Matthew <b>10</b>:1-15 = Mark <b>6</b>:7-13)
and the Mission of the Seventy (Luke <b>10</b>:1-20 =? Matthew<b> 10</b>:16-23).
Now they had better take a sword along (Luke <b>22</b>:36). Doing so was
important enough that anyone without a sword should sell his coat and buy
one—even though at this time of year, it was fairly cold at night in Judaea
(John <b>18</b>:18, 25). Having a sword would give them something to defend
themselves with. A sword was more than a tobacco stick: it represented the
whole gamut of personal defense, civil resistance to evil, and national
protection (Roman <b>13</b>:4).</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:.5in'><span style='font-size:
12.0pt;line-height:115%'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>Most importantly, in this
directive Jesus was talking about the same endeavor his disciples had been taking
part in before: preparing for, establishing, and extending the kingdom of God. They
also constituted continuity in personnel. That continuity of purpose and
personnel means we can look at his later commands and the disciples’ future
efforts to see what buying a sword could and would not mean.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>The disciples’ remark
about having two swords seems a little off the mark. We can sympathize with them,
because they could entertain any number of scenarios since they did not have
the advantage of subsequent experience. They were evidently wrong in their
earlier assumptions (Matthew<b> 16</b>:21-23), but what alternatives remained
were a mystery to them. At any rate, Jesus let it pass with <i>“that is enough”</i>;
having a sword among them would in fact set up a final object lesson for the
group and for the mob that would arrest him.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>A. Not for protecting
Jesus against the arrest and trials</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>            There would have
been little opportunity to buy swords at midnight anyway. When Peter did try to
use his sword to prevent the arrest later that night in Gethsemane, Jesus
stopped him, </span><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>“Put
your sword away. Everybody that takes up a sword dies by a sword” </span></i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(Matthew <b>26</b>:51-53; cp. Luke <b>23</b>:49-51;
John <b>18</b>:10-11; Mark <b>14</b>:47). The next morning the Lord could convincingly
tell Pilate, <i>“My ‘kingdom’ is not an earthly kingdom. If it was, my servants
would be fighting to keep me from being delivered to the Jews” (</i>John <b>18</b>:36).
Besides, Jesus’ admonition about resistance looks forward to more than what
would happen in the morning (Matthew<b> 10</b>:16-23; Mark <b>13</b>:11-13;
Luke<b> 12</b>:21-19).</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>B. Not particularly for
protection against wild animals</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>            No appreciable
difference from earlier missions would exist in the natural surroundings where
they would be operating even though the work would become more wide-ranging and
of longer duration. Swords were not crucial for that purpose during the Mission
of the Twelve and the Seventy, where they were forbidden. Aside from those
endeavors, during Jesus’ ministry two swords had sufficed for Jesus, the twelve
disciples, and several women who traveled along to help with daily needs (Luke <b>22</b>:38).</span><sup><span
style='font-size:16.0pt;line-height:115%'>1</span></sup><span style='font-size:
12.0pt;line-height:115%'> The records give no indication that they ever had to protect
themselves against wild animals. The only time the New Testament mentions wild
animals is with Jesus during his temptation in the unpopulated desert area in
the hills west of the Dead Sea, where John the Baptist grew up (Mark <b>1</b>:13;
Luke <b>1</b>:20). Snakes and scorpions would be the most likely problem in open
country ([Mark <b>16</b>:18]; Acts <b>28</b>:4); but after the return of the
Seventy, Jesus promised protection against being harmed by them at least in the
short term (Luke <b>10</b>:17; cp. Mark <b>16</b>:18).</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>C. Not for establishing
and extending the kingdom</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>            Jesus and the
Twelve had previously been carrying a couple swords along, but they did not use
them for accomplishing their work; so it must have been for protecting
themselves. They had already not been <i>“bearing the sword in vain” </i>(cp.
Romans <b>13</b>:4) nor bearing it to present the kingdom.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>The absence of physical
force holds true across the whole program for establishing and spreading the
kingdom Jesus was preparing the people for: <i>“the kingdom of heaven is at
hand”</i> (Matthew <b>3</b>:2; <b>4</b>:17; <b>10</b>:7; Mark <b>1</b>:15). </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(1) Even as early as
John <b>4</b>:1-38, Jesus told the Samaritan woman that Messiah’s kingdom would
not center in Gerizim or Jerusalem; even though salvation originates through
the Jews, God is a spirit and looks for people to worship him in spirit and
truth (<b>4</b>:23-24). </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(2) Jesus explained that
the kingdom would not come <i>“with observation”</i> (Luke <b>17</b>:20); it would
not be <i>“here or there”</i>; it would not be geographically defined. That was
because <i>“the kingdom of God is ‘interpersonal’” </i>(<i>Conversational
Version</i> for </span><span lang=EL style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>έντὸς</span><span
lang=EL style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'> </span><span lang=EL
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>ὑμῶν</span><span style='font-size:
12.0pt;line-height:115%'>). Other renderings have been <i>“among you,” “within
you<sup>pl</sup>.” </i>The unusual preposition fits with a less usual idea closer
to <i>“between/inter-”</i> as in inter-personal. With that understanding, <i>CV</i>
translates the whole prepositional phrase as <i>“interpersonal.”</i></span><sup><span
style='font-size:16.0pt;line-height:115%'>2</span></sup></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(3) Matthew evidently
used <i>“kingdom of heaven”</i> rather than <i>“kingdom of God”</i> as a
deliberate substitute in exact parallels with other synoptic texts.</span><sup><span
style='font-size:16.0pt;line-height:115%'>3 </span></sup><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>We can take that substitute label as in
clearer contrast to a kingdom in this world, hence, spiritual, transcendent,
social.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(4) The disciples’ work would
continue like their previous mission of the Twelve (Matthew<b> 10</b>:2-15 =
Mark <b>6</b>:7-13 = Luke <b>9</b>:1-6 16-23) and the Seventy (Luke <b>10</b>:1-24
=? Matthew<b> 10</b>:16-23). The circumstances would change, but their method
of operation would not.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(5) The Great Commission
specifies extending the kingdom by teaching: <i>“Go into all the world and make
disciples . . . <u>teaching</u> them to observe . . . .”</i> (Matthew <b>28</b>:18-20;
[cp. Mark <b>16</b>:15-16]). Jesus did not issue a military commission; he sent
his apostles on a campaign of influence, not force. They were to do the kinds
of things that it takes to make disciples.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(6) The apostles were to
speak for Jesus in Jerusalem, Judaea, Samaria, and the far reaches of the earth
(Acts<b> 1</b>:8). The Book of Acts traces that expansion from Jerusalem to
Rome.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>            In that
connection Jesus did <u>not send “weapons of warfare”</u> to conquer rebellious
people (2 Corinthians <b>10</b>:3-4). Instead, he <u>sent the Spirit</u> to
enhance influence as the messengers’ means and manner of operation.</span><sup><span
style='font-size:16.0pt;line-height:115%'>4</span></sup><span style='font-size:
12.0pt;line-height:115%'> The New Testament record credits the Spirit with
doing that in several overlapping ways.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:1.75in;text-align:justify;text-indent:-.25in'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(a) To call to the disciples’ minds what
Jesus had taught them when he worked among them (John <b>14</b>:25-26; <b>16</b>:12-14;
1 John <b>2</b>:20, 27: <u>to aid memory</u>)</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:1.75in;text-align:justify;text-indent:-.25in'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(b) To teach them beyond what he had covered
with them (John <b>14</b>:26; <b>16</b>:12-13: <u>to add information</u>)</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:1.75in;text-align:justify;text-indent:-.25in'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(c) To help them understand what they
did not seem to get very well (John<b> 2</b>:18-22; cp. Matthew<b> 26</b>:61 =
Mark <b>14</b>:58;<b> 27</b>:40 = Mark <b>15</b>:29; Acts <b>6</b>:14: <u>to clarify
understanding</u>)</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:1.75in;text-align:justify;text-indent:-.25in'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(d) To demonstrate through beneficial
<u>miraculous gifts</u> what would help prove the divine origin of the message
Jesus commissioned them to deliver (Acts <b>5</b>:32; [Mark <b>16</b>:17-18; <i>etc.</i>]:
<u>to prove claims</u>)</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:1.75in;text-align:justify;text-indent:-.25in'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>                  The launching of
the kingdom on Pentecost came with miraculous demonstration of the rushing,
mighty wind; the tongues of fire above the apostles’ heads, and hearing these
unschooled commoners speak in foreign languages from all over the Roman empire.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:1.75in;text-align:justify;text-indent:-.25in'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(e) To give the messengers wisdom
that their opponents could not confute (Acts <b>4</b>:8; <b>6</b>:10: <u>to
strengthen presenters’ abilities</u>)</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:1.75in;text-align:justify;text-indent:-.25in'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(f) To give his representatives a
boldness that met the opposition to their message (Acts <b>4</b>:13-23, 29-31: <u>to
embolden the witnesses</u>)</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:1.75in;text-align:justify;text-indent:-.25in'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(g) To guide the missionaries in
their travels to bear work and witness sometimes with an angel of the Lord
(Acts <b>8</b>:26-40;<b> 10</b>:17ff + 11:1ff;<b> 11</b>:27-30; <b>14</b>:26;
13:4; <b>16</b>:6, 7-<u>13</u>-15</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:1.75in;text-align:justify;text-indent:-.25in'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(h) To provide them with responses to
charges they would face when put on trial (Matthew <b>10</b>:19-20; Luke <b>12</b>:11-12;
Acts <b>4</b>:8-12; <b>6</b>:8-10; <b>13</b>:9-12: <u>to quicken the spokesmen’s
minds</u>)</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:1.75in;text-align:justify;text-indent:-.25in'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(i)  To set examples for people to
follow (1 Corinthians <b>4</b>:16; <b>11</b>:1; Philippians <b>3</b>:17;<b> 4</b>:9;
1 Thessalonians <b>1</b>:6; 2 Thessalonians<b> 3</b>:9)</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(7) Paul characterizes
the Messianic mission as <i>reconciliation</i> in 2 Corinthians <b>5</b>:18-19:</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify'><b><sup><span style='font-size:14.0pt;
line-height:115%'>18</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt;
line-height:115%'>“[God] has reconciled us to himself through Christ and has
given us a ministry about reconciliation </span></i><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt;line-height:115%'>19</span></sup></b><i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>that in Christ, God was reconciling
the world to himself, not holding their trespasses against them, and putting in
us the message about reconciliation. </span></i><b><sup><span style='font-size:
14.0pt;line-height:115%'>20</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt;
line-height:115%'>We’re ambassadors for Christ, and God is making his appeal to
the world through us. Please, for the sake of Christ, be reconciled to God.”</span></i></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='text-align:justify;text-indent:1.0in'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>These notations on setting up the
kingdom demonstrate the theory behind the process. Establishing and extending
the kingdom illustrate a fundamental fact about causality: <u>manner and means
conform to purpose</u>. The nature of a process becomes evident in the nature
of its result. Cause and effect lie in the same category; even Genesis <b>1</b>
repeatedly pictures the principle as <i>“after their kind.”</i> Scripture
states the concept elsewhere under wording like <i>“People don’t gather grapes
from thorns or figs from thistles, do they?” </i>(Matthew <b>7</b>:16; Mark <b>11</b>:13;
Luke <b>6</b>:44, John <b>3</b>:6; James <b>3</b>:11-12; cp 1 Corinthians<b> 15</b>:50);
<i>“What you sow you reap” </i>(Galatians<b> 5</b>:7-8). No wonder the kingdom
originated and spread like it did—by influence through teaching, preaching, witnessing,
explaining, setting example, doing good, ministering, reasoning with their
audiences (Acts <b>17</b>:2; <b>18</b>:4, 14; <b>24</b>:25), appealing, being
ambassadors. The kingdom is interpersonal, so it originated and grows by
interpersonal manner and means.</span><sup><span style='font-size:16.0pt;
line-height:115%'>5</span></sup></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>            D. For self-protection</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>            Now comes the
conclusion in regard to pacifism. <i>“But now”</i> (</span><span lang=EL
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>ἀλλὰ</span><span lang=EL
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'> </span><span lang=EL
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>νῦν</span><span style='font-size:
12.0pt;line-height:115%'>) does not indicate a change of method to prevent
Jesus’ arrest and trials, nor a need to protect against dangers in the wild, nor
a shift in the manner and means of establishing and spreading the kingdom (John
<b>16</b>:5). That leaves self-protection as the reason for carrying a sword.
The same purpose of mission obtained as before, <i>“but now”</i> came a need
for a sword for protection during the work, not for doing the work.
Furthermore, a sword is not a switch; it can wound, maim, and kill as necessary.
Jesus’ comments leave lesser self-protections open to discretion: physically
restraining, overwhelming, disabling, besides contacting authorities when
possible.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>The change must come in
response to increased hostility. What would cause that altered circumstance?
The Mission of the Twelve and the Seventy took place during “the period of
consideration” as to whether Jesus was the prophet (Deuteronomy <b>18</b>:12,
18), Elijah, another returning prophet (Matthew <b>16</b>:14; <b>17</b>:10;
Mark <b>6</b>:15; Luke <b>9</b>:7-8), or perhaps the Messiah (Matthew <b>11</b>:2-6
= Luke <b>7</b>:18-23; <b>26</b>:43; John <b>7</b>:26-42). </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>But as his ministry
progressed, it became evident that (a) he did not offer what the masses thought
Messiah would do. That hostility eventually boiled over under the aegis of the Jewish
leadership. </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(b) Along the way there
was growing dissatisfaction with him as Messiah, with what he claimed, and what
he taught. He was from the wrong place to be Messiah (Nazareth rather than
Bethlehem: John <b>7</b>:41-43,52, <i>etc</i>.). He spoke of this death (John <b>3</b>:4;
<b>2</b>:8, <i>etc</i>.) and refused to abide by the tradition of the elders on
sabbath observances (Matthew <b>12</b>:14-, <i>etc</i>.), ceremonial defilement
(Matthew <b>15</b>:1-20, <i>etc</i>.), regularized fasting (Matthew <b>9</b>:14-17,
<i>etc</i>.); apartheid with Samaritans, Gentiles, and common people (Matthew<b>
9</b>:10-13, <i>etc</i>.); and several other points. </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(c) He said things the general
public as well as the religious leaders took exception to: forgiving sin
(Matthew <b>9</b>:1-8), speaking against the temple (Matthew <b>26</b>:60-61, <i>etc</i>.),
supposedly going beyond the Law in his injunctions (Matthew <b>5</b>:17ff; cp.
Acts <b>6</b>:11), and the like.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(d) Some claims did not
make sense to them: eating his flesh and drinking his blood (John <b>6</b>:51-59),
claiming to have come down from heaven when they knew is parents (John <b>6</b>:38,
58), promising that his followers would never die (John <b>8</b>:51-53), claiming
to have seen Abraham (John <b>8</b>:56-59), and other matters.</span><sup><span
style='font-size:16.0pt;line-height:115%'>6</span></sup></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>(e) With <u>the death of
the leader</u>, opponents would be emboldened against his disciples with more than
earlier verbal challenges. Killing Jesus removed a major reason for accepting
him as Messiah; only by convincing the masses that Jesus had resurrected could
his witnesses have any hope of convincing the masses that he was the Messiah.
The crucial truth was that <i>“Messiah abides forever”</i> (John<b> 12</b>:34).
The patriotic spirit and religious sense of national identity would increasingly
exert itself till a generation later the Romans would destroy the Jewish
state—as the Book of Acts records time and again. That is also why the
resurrection of Jesus had to play such a prominent part in the proclamation
about him from Pentecost forward (in the <i>kerygma</i>: Acts <b>2</b>). For
the Jews, a dead Messiah was a contradiction of terms; they had not been expecting
a suffering Messiah (John <b>12</b>:34; Luke <b>24</b>:25-27, <i>etc</i>.). </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>Shaking the dust off
their feet and running off to another town would no longer be enough for the
evangelists’ safety. In more private circumstances, having a sword could at
least deter unarmed attackers and citizens taking action on their own (John<b>
16</b>:21). A sword might dissuade critics from “roughing up” the evangelists
at a personal level. Even just displaying a sword in its sheath would have a dampening
effect on physical opposition.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>Of course, using the
sword would have limited value against the stronger opposition these workmen
would face. There was still the cross that each one had committed themselves to
pick up and carry to their own crucifixion (Matthew <b>10</b>:38; <b>16</b>:24-26).
Official opposition reinforced by authority and force would still bring them
into synagogues as local courts for trials and scourgings (Matthew <b>10</b>:16-20;
34-37). Family members, friends, and neighbors would have access to authorities
for getting disciples imprisoned or even executed (Matthew <b>10</b>:21). We
suspect that Saul’s attempt to systematically wipe out the church could have
been devastating if the Lord had not intervened to bring about his conversion (Acts
<b>9</b>:1-25; <b>21</b>:27-<b>26</b>:32ff. + Philippians <b>3</b>:6; 1 Timothy
<b>1</b>:13). After he became a disciple, the church in Judaea, Samaria, and
Galilee had peace (Acts <b>9</b>:31).</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>Admittedly, under such
circumstances fighting back with a weapon would be pointless; fleeing to
another city perhaps outside the country might be an option (Matthew <b>10</b>:23).
Later, after the stoning of Stephen, disciples dispersed even to foreign cities,
turning persecution into evangelism (Acts <b>8</b>:4-8). </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
1.0in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>Nevertheless, for
present purposes, using a sword for self-protection or defending a traveling
partner had approval and even advocacy from the Lord. That leaves dealing with
the passage below that advocates use to argue for non-violent response.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:6.0pt;text-align:justify'><b><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>II. Matthew 5:38-42</span></b></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>This paragraph in the
Sermon on the Mount has served as the basis for the various forms of
pacificism. Properly reading Jesus’ comments depends on two features of context—general
then specific: (1) the Sermon addresses social relations as organized
conceptually around the Golden Rule (<b>7</b>:12); and (2) Jesus corrects a longstanding,
popular misuse of the Mosaic <u>judicial</u> principle <i>“eye for an eye.”</i>
Applying it to <u>social</u> relations transforms it into justification for
revenge.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>We start with the
second point. In three places the Mosaic Law directs judges on handling violent
crimes. In a case where men fighting cause a woman to miscarry, Exodus <b>21</b>:22-25
says, </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify;text-indent:.5in;line-height:normal'><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt'>22</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>“If
men are fighting and hit a pregnant woman and she miscarries without further
injury, fine him as her husband requires and the judges determine. </span></i><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt'>23</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>But
if there’s further harm, punish<sup>f</sup> life for life, </span></i><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt'>24</span></sup></b><b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>eye
for eye, tooth for tooth </span></i></b><b><span style='font-size:9.0pt'>[Mt 5:38]</span></b><i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>, hand for hand, foot for foot, </span></i><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt'>25</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>burn
for burn, wound for wound, bruise for bruise.” </span></i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>[</span><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>Conversational
Version</span></i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>]</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:0in;text-align:justify;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:
12.0pt'>Speaking of citizens and aliens alike, Leviticus <b>24</b>:20 says, </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify;text-indent:.5in;line-height:normal'><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt'>19</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>“If
one person injures another, do the same thing to that person: </span></i><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt'>20</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>fracture
for fracture, </span></i><b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>eye for eye,
tooth for tooth</span></i></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'> </span></i><b><span
style='font-size:9.0pt'>[Mt 5:38]</span></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>.</span></i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>” [<i>CV</i>]</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:0in;text-align:justify;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:
12.0pt'>Deuteronomy</span><b><span style='font-size:12.0pt'> 19</span></b><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>:21 broadens the point to cases of false witnesses, </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify;line-height:normal'><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt'>                  16</span></sup></b><i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>“If a malicious witness accuses someone, </span></i><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt'>17</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>both
people will stand in the presence of the </span></i><i><span style='font-size:
12.0pt'>L</span></i><i><span style='font-size:9.0pt'>ORD</span></i><i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>, </span></i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>that is, <i>in
front of the priests and judges in office. </i></span><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt'>18</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>The
judges will investigate thoroughly. If the witness has accused falsely, </span></i><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt'>19</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>do
to him what he’d intended to do to the fellow citizen. That’s how you’re to
purge sin from you. </span></i><b><sup><span style='font-size:14.0pt'>20</span></sup></b><i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>The rest </span></i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>of
the people <i>will show respect and not do such a sinful thing. </i></span><b><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt'>21</span></sup></b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>Don’t
show mercy: life for life, <b>eye for eye, tooth for tooth </b></span></i><b><span
style='font-size:9.0pt'>[Mt 5:38]</span></b><b><i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>,</span></i></b><i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'> hand for hand, foot for foot.”</span></i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'> [<i>CV</i>]</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;line-height:
normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>When applied to personal relations, the
words of Moses sound like the L</span><span style='font-size:10.0pt'>ORD</span><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'> permits people to <i>“get even”</i> on all kinds of
slights and minor disabuses. They might even do that instead of calling the
authorities to take care of more significant matters. </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>Jesus admonished
against that misapplication. Instead of wanting to fight <i>“at the drop of a
hat,”</i> they were to, so to speak, <i>“turn the other cheek.” </i>Instead of
trying to <i>“get back at”</i> someone, <i>“go the second mile.” </i>That mentality
derives from the Golden Rule, something especially noticeable in the parallel
“Sermon on the Plain” (Luke <b>6</b>:20-38). There in the layout of the text, the
Rule contrasts immediately with <i>“eye for an eye”</i> (<b>6</b>:27-30 + <u>31</u>).
Around the Rule, Luke’s gospel clusters several of the same correctives that
Matthew <b>5-7</b> records. The Rule describes a pattern of behavior aside from
any feelings people might have toward their enemies.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>The revenge mentality
perpetuates social division and makes it worse because people tend to get
“evener.” <u>The first aspect of genius in Jesus’ teaching taps into people’s
tendency to respond in kind</u>. Instead of doing so with evil, his Golden Rule
asks how we want people to treat us and says to act <u>that</u> way instead of
the way the other has treated us. That establishes two related correctives: (a)
it halts the pattern of deteriorating associations, and (b) it tends to rebuild
relationships because, again, people tend to respond in kind—in this case to our
love pattern of behavior. The same “responding in kind” that destroys, Jesus draws
on to correct and create. Obviously, to break the downward pattern, at some
point doing good must respond to evil, a fact commanded repeatedly in the New
Testament: Romans<b> 12</b>:9-21; 1 Thessalonians <b>5</b>:15; 1 Peter<b> 2</b>:23;
<b>3</b>:9. That is what Jesus calls on his disciples to do.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>As the Old Testament
texts used overt behaviors to make tangible the principle that judges were to
apply, so Jesus uses some tangible behaviors to illustrate his point: <i>turning
the other cheek, giving a litigant our coat too, going the second mile, giving
to someone in need.</i> <u>A second aspect of genius in Jesus’ teaching is that
by turning the other cheek and going the second mile, we take charge of the
situation.</u> Instead of following an agenda set by an offender, <u>we</u> set
the agenda—with a view to disarming evil.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>In all of this, Jesus
was not correcting the Law; he was correcting a misappropriation of it (<b>5</b>:17).
He reworded the Second Great Commandment (Leviticus<b> 19</b>:18) to establish a
mentality and behavior pattern appropriate to the interpersonal circumstance: </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify;text-indent:.5in;line-height:normal'><i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>“Love / neighbor / as self”          </span></i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>becomes </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify;text-indent:.5in;line-height:normal'><i><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>“do    / to others  / as you would have others do to
you.”</span></i><span style='font-size:12.0pt'> </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>Like almost every
negative behavior, vengeance comes significantly from a desire to reinforce
self-esteem. People are wired to feel valuable, so they try to enhance their
sense of self-worth. They often chose to do that by trying to succeed in
competition, which makes them feel “good” by being “better than.” That
self-defeating method works only for a few and only for a while. Love via the
Golden Rule is the closest thing to a universal social “solvent,” treating
other people the way we want to be treated helps cure the enemy’s deficiency.
So not responding in kind (love) puts victims in charge and changes enemies by
eroding the root cause of their sin. Turning the other cheek helps cure
vengeance.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>These verses bear on the
kinds of pacifism that interpreters infer from them. First, Jesus’ examples are
relatively minor. They do not correspond to the severity of the ten or so examples
the Mosaic legislation gives. Jesus’ illustrations sound more like personal
affronts as in <i>“but words will never hurt me.”</i> Jesus’ words encourage us
to <i>“be above all that,”</i> <i>“to let it go,”</i> not to react to <i>“petty
stuff” </i>people pull to <i>“get at”</i> us. Letting someone slap me in the
face is a far cry from letting them break my neck, letting a bully beat up a
member of my family, not stopping an attacker from taking my life and leaving my
family without a mother, not protecting my people when evil mobs try to take
over our land. Stopping such evil requires more than talk, counseling, standing
in the way, absorbing punches, running away, calling the police. Law
enforcement may not be available; nothing short of lethal force personally
exercised will suffice in some situations; that is what using a sword must include.
Pacifism does not work for individuals or society. </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>Jesus’ interpersonal illustrations
speak more to a mentality than to specifics, much less to letting someone wound,
maim, and kill us or someone near us. Since turning the other cheek contrasts
with violent crimes that <i>eye for an eye</i> envisions, Jesus is not
contemplating response to such violent acts when he says to <i>turn the other
cheek</i>. He is responding to the misuse of those words in everyday social
interaction; consequently, he is not advocating pacifism as normally meant. This
last element in interpretation invokes the “law of opposition”: a statement
means what it contrasts with. </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify;text-indent:.5in;line-height:normal'><b><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>(a) Jesus’ commandment contrasts with the <u>social</u>
misapplication of the Mosaic injunction, not with the Mosaic <u>judicial</u> injunction
itself. </span></b></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify;text-indent:.5in;line-height:normal'><b><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>(b) In addition to Matthew 5 not dealing with offenses
of bodily harm, </span></b></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify;text-indent:.5in;line-height:normal'><b><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>(c) turning the other cheek contrasts with social
vengeance, not with physical force. Refraining from using physical force lies outside
Jesus’ frame of reference. Besides, </span></b></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify;line-height:normal'><b><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>          *(e) <u>revenge happens after an offence in
response to it; self-protection occurs at the time of an offence to prevent it.</u>
Furthermore,</span></b></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:.5in;margin-bottom:0in;
margin-left:.5in;text-align:justify;text-indent:.5in;line-height:normal'><b><span
style='font-size:12.0pt'>(f) self-protection means not acting in “my own name”
but acting in the absence of official protection, in the absence of
representatives of the law, who are normally the ones who do not act in their
own name but in the name of the law.</span></b></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>Even aside from Jesus’ words,
we agree to not using force beyond what protecting a person from physical or
emotional abuse calls for. We even agree with restraining police from using
excessive force even though God has constituted human government for that very
purpose (Romans <b>13</b>:1-7; Titus <b>3</b>:1; 1 Peter <b>2</b>:13-17).   </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>Purely interpersonal
situations bleed off into the executive branch of government and on into
complicated international issues including military operations in “just war.”
Without specifically addressing that final level here, we note that we live in more
than the interpersonal realm; some of us also have to man the police force and supply
personnel for military duty. John the Baptist did not tell soldiers to quit
their military assignments (Luke <b>3</b>:14) or government workers to give up collecting
taxes (Luke <b>3</b>:12-13). </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>As to pacifism, two
significant points apply: in police and national service, physical force must operate,
but the personnel do not use it in their own name. That likewise applies in
self-defense and defense of others, so individuals fulfill the need an absent “authority”
would otherwise fulfill. They do so, not of themselves but as surrogates for
unavailable resources, not because they want to, but because they have to for necessary
purposes; otherwise, there would be no “law and order” whether they were
dealing with friend or foe, privately or internationally. The mentality Jesus
advocates in Matthew <b>5 </b>is what operates in “not acting in our own name.”</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>The conclusion about
self-defense and pacifism combines Jesus’ words in Luke <b>22</b>:35-38 and
Matthew <b>5</b>:38-42. (1) Using a sword sets a limit on turning the other
cheek; and, so to speak, turning the cheek sets a limit on “using the sword.” They
both speak to a mentality even more than to specifics like the ones Jesus
mentions. The Luke passage addressed a more hostile setting in the future; Matthew
addressed the purely social setting at any time. The first admonished buying a
sword (self-protection); the second admonished not seeking revenge (a
mentality).  </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>(2) Putting the
admonitions in balance also helps keep the other person “honest.” It is not good
to let bullies persist in “slapping ‘me’ around,” to let lazy people keep asking
for handouts instead of working (2 Thessalonians <b>3</b>:11-12), to let people
“take advantage of” my willingness to help, or to “let them off the hook” for not
fulfilling their responsibilities: <i>“Every person is to bear their own
burden” </i>(Galatians <b>6</b>:5) even while it is true that <i>“we are to
bear one another’s burdens and so fulfill the law of Christ”</i> (Galatians <b>6</b>:2).
Without feeling guilty or unfaithful, “I” can prioritize my responsibilities,
my family’s need for my time and attention, and so on.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>(3) Even more broadly, “I”
can evaluate what is appropriate given the complex of variables every case involves.
Balancing Matthew <b>5</b> and Luke<b> 22</b> opens up our using “sliding-scale
judgment” between people and along the way with individual people as they
change. “I” can be more willing to let other people’s actions “slide” whose mental
conditions, emotional strength, abilities, background, and opportunities are not
below normal. “I” can exercise greater patience with people that are showing
progress. “I” can judge whether the seventh of “77 repentances” is genuine (Matthew
<b>18</b>:22-35). Besides, we cannot always be sure how to respond even when we
prioritize an enemy’s welfare. Sometimes even opposite responses may be morally
appropriate as referenced to “my” motive and attitude in given situations. In
other words, even with Matthew and Luke in balance, we may not follow a set
pattern across all persons or continuously with the same person; real
situations have too many features for static verdicts. Such factors issue from
the complexities of relationships between persons.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in;line-height:normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>Jesus advocated a
mentality in Matthew (subjective) as balanced in Luke by the real world we face
in life and ministry (objective). Those inclinations lean on each other for
good whether for us or our enemies</span></p>
