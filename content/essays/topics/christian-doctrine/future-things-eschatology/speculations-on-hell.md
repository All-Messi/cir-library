<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>We do not know fully what
aspects of earthly humanity carry forward into the intermediate and eternal
states, so we are left to speculate about what nature we will have then and what
punitive experiences we can experience there. The following inferences combine what
revelation does say about permanent features of human nature and about being
“lost” after death—about what hell could be like.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>A. God created us with
the <u>interpersonal capacity</u>, a characteristic we infer from his making us
in his image and likeness (Genesis <b>1</b>:26). So whatever else constitutes
“destruction,” it can take something away from operating interpersonally. Destruction
would not take away the interpersonal capacity, only the free exercise of it. Destruction
would not having take away our existence itself either by withdrawing sustaining
our existence or by annihilation. Continued existence combines with
“destruction,” as scripture makes clear by speaking o (1) persons being (2) in
hell. Hell takes place in regard to the overlap between the natural and
supernatural realms, particularly in regard to the interpersonal capacity in
that area.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>B. We can appeal to <u>poetic
justice</u> as a principle for understanding how hell operates. Hell derives
from human sinfulness during physical life (Luke <b>16</b>:25, 26-31). Poetic
justice calls for making the punishment commensurate with the crime (Luke <b>12</b>:46-49).
It seems appropriate to put onto criminals what they put onto their victims. So
any blame involved in the situation does not fall on God as judge, jury, and
“executioner” but on those who disobey him. Our final condition derives from
our human response to God’s revealed will and warnings about not heeding it.
Even aside from revelation, God can hold us responsible for what we should be
able to figure out on our own, given human experience itself (Luke<b> 12</b>:48a). 
</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>C. Justice seems to
require the <u>permanent overthrow of evil</u>. Hell serves as the final triumph
over sinners and past sin (Revelation <b>14</b>:11a). It is punitive, not remedial,
so it is permanent with no transfer from hell to some other circumstance (Luke <b>16</b>:26).</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>From these three basics
we infer the following possibilities for how hell may operate in the context of
its revealed features.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>1. <u>Hell as eternal
loneliness</u>. In criminal incarceration, prisons may enhance punishment by
using solitary confinement. That condition “destroys” social interaction; it
takes away the exercise of the interpersonal capacity. It compares hell to
separation from God (Isaiah <b>59</b>:2). Separation also from other people recognizes
sin as self-centeredness, anti-social behavior; it behaves in ways that do not
consider the impact of “my” actions on “you,” human or divine. Sin as
selfishness means receiving in hell what essentially we express in sinning
against others. We can imagine how there will be degrees of punishment implicit
in conscious loneliness. “Outer darkness” would intensify solitary confinement
and perhaps depict “destruction” of perception during consciousness.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>2. <u>Hell as observing
the effects of our unforgiven sin</u>. What if our sentence is to witness the
sorrow and suffering our actions have caused other people? We could hardly
complain if we had to watch a replay of each sin as it affected each person. The
degree of punishment could correspond to the number and intensity of our disobedience
to known and knowable prohibitions (Luke <b>12</b>:47-48).</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>3. <u>Hell as
experiencing the effects of our unforgiven sins</u>.</span><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt;line-height:115%'>1</span></sup><span style='font-size:
12.0pt;line-height:115%'> Insensitivity to others is an aspect of selfishness
and self-centeredness, which breach the very nature of interpersonal behavior.
Going through what we have caused others to endure provides something appropriate
and commensurate. This speculation may even suggest duration of our sins’
effects. We could be made to endure the length and degree of the kind of sorrow
and pain we brought on other people by our mental, emotional, and physical
abuse. God could measure out to us sequentially for each person in the group what
our meanness exploited each time. </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>Such a scenario would not
transfer guilt or its consequences (Ezekiel <b>18</b>:20); it would activate the
consequences on us who did the sin, and it would incur punishment for it in kind,
degree, and duration. It would not be a <u>supposedly comparable substitute
effect</u> but the very effect itself. It would not, for instance, be a
monetary fine for attacking a woman; it would be the actual fear and pain she
felt in being attacked. It would be <u>actually feeling</u> a man’s grief over the
loss of the son we murdered, the other family members’ grief for that same
murder measured out to the murderer one at a time together with the sense of
loss per individual for that same death. </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>Woe to those who foment
war, suffering, grief, pain, and death against thousands, those who bring
starvation on children and destroy the environment with selfishness or in
pursuit of personal gain this side of their own grave. If that is what hell
will be like, it will be <u>a condition of their own making.</u> Violent people
would not “get off easy”; they would not “get off” at all, and God could not be
criticized for their excruciating condition because they would be experiencing
the very excruciating effects that their own actions brought on neighbors and
friends. </span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>Our sins bring
disappointment to other people; having to experience such a thing could even introduce
an <u>element of duration</u> because people’s disappointment and
discouragement can linger for life.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>This speculation could
include punishment as a <u>constant experience</u>, where we would have <i>“no
rest day or night”</i> (Revelation <b>14</b>:11). Being outside the features of
physical experience, not even sleep would offer periodic reprieve.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>A question of consistency
comes up before we leave these thoughts about the nature of resurrected body
and the possibility of its corporeal suffering. If our resurrected condition
means our “bodies” are not naturally susceptible to physical pain, then
corporeally punishing those who corporeally made others suffer needs some
attention. Such punitive treatment would not come onto everybody
indiscriminately but onto those who harmed others physically. The punishment
would be done <u>positively</u>, <u>specialized</u> to each person for <u>each way</u>
they hurt their fellows, and calibrated for <u>intensity</u> and <u>duration</u>.
The whole idea might seem suspect by “dying a death of thousand qualifications,”
but God can do all these thousands of things, and under these modifications
would not incur blame. However he inflicts the punishment will involve all these
qualifiers with or without sensitivity to corporeal pain; that inheres in the
very notion of commensurate justice itself.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>4. <u>Hell as observing
heaven from afar</u>. This speculation does a take-off on one element of
scenery in the Rich Man and Lazarus (Luke<b> 16</b>:23). Our “punishment” would
come from being continually confronted with the loss of what we thrust away by
our sin. By refusing to reach out in love, we lost the echo effect that love
triggers in friends.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>5. <u>Hell as confined
inactivity</u>. Such a circumstance would “destroy” exercising the will in
behavior, volition being a primary factor in personhood. This possibility draws
on limitation and boredom as forms of punishment. The spirits in prison from
the days of Noah had no prospect of parole (1 Peter <b>3</b>:18-20).</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>A follow-up note suggests
that such hypotheticals as we have envisioned here could combine in various
ways or occur in sequence. Taking away the questionable idea of a burning hell
does not make it a wonderful place. At the same time, we remove a source of
doubt among believers and irrelevant criticism of the Christian worldview among
disbelievers.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify'><span
style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>The speculations above
attempt to sidestep the discontinuous images that describe hell in the Bible: (a)
darkness in the cold outside a comfortable house filled with celebration (Matthew
<b>9</b>:42, <i>etc</i>.), (b) burning fire and magots in a city dump—Gehenna being
a representation of the abode of the godless dead (Mark <b>9</b>:48), and (c) a
flaming lava lake fuming with sulfur (Revelation <b>20</b>:10). We understand
these as pictures that make a sobering point metaphorically.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;text-indent:
.5in'><span style='font-size:12.0pt;line-height:115%'>These speculations are
indeed speculations, but we offer them to enhance a sense of reality about hell.
They draw attention to the effects of our negative behaviors and admonish us to
stop doing them. They offer alternatives to (a) the seeming impropriety of a personal
<u>God</u> torturing persons indiscriminately, eternally, and perhaps equally—the
predominant image of hell that people seem to draw from scripture.</span><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt;line-height:115%'>2</span></sup><span style='font-size:
12.0pt;line-height:115%'> That image, taken physically, (b) does not seem
appropriate in kind, degree, or length for <u>justice or punishment</u> of differing
flesh-and-blood sinners who sin temporarily. (c) A burning hell seems dissociated
from spirit as something naturally susceptible to fire (Judges <b>13</b>:20).</span><sup><span
style='font-size:14.0pt;line-height:115%'>3</span></sup><span style='font-size:
12.0pt;line-height:115%'> (d) The mixture of discontinuous images suggests not
taking them all literally. Again, we say that outer darkness does not mix with
flaming fire, and flaming fire does not mix with living magots; but that
admission does not make hell unreal or something we need not worry about
because it wouldn’t be “all that bad” if we end up there. There are still those
interpersonal capacities that can experience penalty.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:0in;

style='font-size:14.0pt'>3</span></sup><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>This
essay supposes that resurrection gives us something markedly different from our
present physical body. It seems to be more than the resuscitation of a dead
corpse; translation or transfiguration apparently will accompany it (Matthew <b>17</b>:2-3;
Mark <b>9</b>:2-4; Luke <b>9</b>:29-30). The Apostle Paul addresses the
question in 1 Corinthians <b>15</b>:35-54. He describes the resurrected body as
a heavenly, immortal, celestial, incorruptible, spiritual body that differs
from our present body like a grain of wheat differs from the plant that grows
from it—continuity of identity with change of constitution. That variance
involves not marrying or possessing sexuality as Jesus told the Sadducees
(Matthew <b>22</b>:30-32). As a group, the apostle’s descriptors seem to
indicate a non-corporeal entity that would not feel physical torture as by
fire. Those features make resurrection more than the immortality of the soul, a
belief among the Greeks that left them rejecting resurrection (Acts <b>17</b>:32).
At the same time, our speculations admittedly would involve corporeal
experience if we were to feel the physical suffering that we have caused other
people; those punishments would have to come by positive action on God’s part.
At least such suffering would seem justified!</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;line-height:
normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>            This picture leaves unaddressed
the incidents in Jesus’ resurrection appearances where he appeared in a locked
room, ate fish, showed the “healed” scars from his crucifixion and the final
spear thrust, could be touched, and could ascend into the sky. The apologetic
element in those appearances complicates comparing his and our resurrected conditions
and what punishments they might make natural.</span></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:0in;text-align:justify;line-height:
normal'><span style='font-size:12.0pt'>            If non-corporeality in the
resurrection proves to be a false inference, that fact neutralizes some
comments in #3 above and in the final paragraph of main text. The other
observations still stand, however, in speculations about punishment in hell
that is not endless torture for everyone alike.</span></p>
